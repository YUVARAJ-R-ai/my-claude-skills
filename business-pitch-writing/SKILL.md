# Business Pitch Writing

A pitch's job is not to describe a business. It's to make one specific person — an investor, a customer, a judge — feel like they'd be missing out if they said no. Most pitches fail not because the idea is weak, but because the writing buries a good idea under jargon, hedging, and feature lists that could describe any company.

Everything below exists to fight that. Adapt it to the situation rather than following it as a checklist — a hackathon pitch and a seed-round one-pager need the same honesty and clarity, but not the same length or tone.

## Step 0: Know what you're writing before you write it

Three things change everything else about the pitch. If the person hasn't told you, infer the most likely answer from context and say so in one line before proceeding — don't stall on a clarifying question for something this can default sensibly:

1. **Audience** — investor, customer, partner, competition judges, or a hiring manager all want to hear a different "why this matters to me."
2. **Format** — a 30-second spoken pitch, a one-page written document, or slide-by-slide deck narration are different writing tasks with different length budgets (see the cheat sheet below).
3. **The one sentence they should be able to repeat back** — if you can't state this in a single plain sentence before drafting, the pitch will wander. Nail this first, then build outward.

If the person hands you an existing pitch to fix rather than asking for one from scratch, treat their facts, numbers, and claims as fixed. Rewrite for clarity and pull, but never invent traction figures, funding amounts, customer counts, or competitive claims they didn't give you — flag them as placeholders instead (`[insert current user count]`) so they get filled with something true.

## The structure

Not every pitch needs every beat, and a 30-second pitch obviously compresses several into one line — but this is the order that reliably works, and the reason each piece earns its place:

1. **Hook** — a sharp statistic, a one-line story, or a bold, specific claim. Its only job is to make the next sentence get read. Communications coach Carmine Gallo's rule of thumb: if you can't say what you do in about 15 seconds, you've already lost the room.
2. **Problem** — specific and concrete, not "the market lacks efficient solutions." Make the reader recognize the pain, ideally through a real scenario rather than an abstract claim.
3. **Solution** — what you actually built or offer, stated plainly enough that a smart stranger gets it on the first read.
4. **Why now** — what changed recently that makes this the right moment (new technology, new regulation, a shift in behavior). This is what separates "good idea" from "good idea, and here's why it wasn't built already."
5. **How it works** — just enough mechanism to be credible, not a technical deep-dive. One diagram or one sentence, not a spec sheet.
6. **Business model** — how money actually moves. Investors and partners will not take this on faith.
7. **Traction / proof** — real numbers, a testimonial, a pilot result, a waitlist. If there's genuinely none yet, lean on the team and the insight instead of manufacturing false urgency.
8. **Differentiation** — what makes this different from the obvious alternatives (including "doing nothing"). Never claim "no competition" — it reads as naive, not confident. Name the real alternative and say why this beats it.
9. **Team** — why these specific people can pull this off, in one credibility-establishing line each, not a full bio.
10. **The ask** — a specific number and a specific next step ("$250K to hit 10K users by Q3" beats "we're raising a seed round"). A pitch that ends vaguely wastes everything that came before it.

For slide decks specifically, Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule is a useful discipline even if you never present live: roughly ten slides, one idea per slide, one bold takeaway rather than a wall of text. It forces you to find the point instead of hiding behind detail.

## The language rules — this is where "attractive" and "jargon-free" actually happen

Jargon doesn't sound sophisticated to the reader — it sounds like a wall they have to climb to figure out what you actually do, and busy readers just... don't climb it. Every rule here is really the same rule: say the specific, true thing in the shortest plain sentence that carries it.

- **Read it aloud.** If a sentence sounds like it belongs in a press release, rewrite it the way you'd actually explain the idea to a friend at dinner.
- **Numbers beat adjectives.** "Cuts onboarding from three weeks to two days" does more work than "significantly improves efficiency." If you don't have the number, that's a sign to go find it, not to reach for a bigger adjective.
- **One idea per sentence.** Stacked clauses are where jargon hides.
- **Active voice, concrete nouns.** "We built X" beats "X was developed leveraging Y."
- **Retire the hype words.** *Revolutionary, innovative, disruptive, cutting-edge, world-class, best-in-class, synergy, leverage, ecosystem, paradigm shift, next-generation, seamless, robust, unlock value, game-changing.* None of these are false exactly — they're just so overused that investors and readers now skim right past them. Replace each with the specific thing it's standing in for.

A few concrete swaps:

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| Leverage synergies | Work together to do more |
| Disruptive innovation | A cheaper / faster / simpler way to do X |
| Best-in-class, scalable platform | It works reliably and grows with you |
| Unlock value for stakeholders | Save (or make) customers money |
| Seamless integration | Plugs into the tools they already use, no setup |
| Cutting-edge AI-powered ecosystem | An AI that does [specific thing] |
| Mission-critical solution | Something they can't run the business without |
| Robust go-to-market strategy | How we'll actually find our first customers |

**Worked example:**

Input (jargon-heavy): *"Our platform leverages a cutting-edge, AI-powered ecosystem to deliver a best-in-class, scalable solution that unlocks value for enterprise stakeholders through seamless, mission-critical integrations."*

Output (plain, attractive): *"Sales teams lose about six hours a week copying data between five different tools. Our app does that syncing automatically, in the background, so reps spend the time actually selling. It plugs into the tools they already use — setup takes ten minutes, not a procurement cycle."*

Nothing was added except specificity. That's usually the whole fix.

## Tell it as a story, not a spec sheet

The strongest pitches put the *customer* in the hero role and the business in the role of the thing that helps them win — not the founder as protagonist. A useful shape: show the world as it frustratingly is right now, let the reader feel the friction, then show the world as it could be once the problem is solved. The pitch is the bridge between those two, and the solution section is where you hand the reader across it. This is also why a short, real anecdote about one specific user often lands harder than a paragraph of aggregate stats — people remember people, not percentages.

## Format cheat sheet

- **Elevator pitch** (~75–100 words, 30–45 seconds spoken): hook, problem, solution, ask. Nothing else fits — cut ruthlessly rather than rushing the delivery.
- **One-pager** (roughly 400–600 words, one physical page): headline + one-sentence value prop at the top, then short paragraphs or tight bullets through the structure above, ending in a clear ask. Assume the reader gives it 30 seconds before deciding whether to keep reading.
- **Pitch deck narration** (~10 slides): one idea per slide, plain language even on the technical slides (see the AI example above — describe the mechanism in a sentence a non-technical investor would repeat back correctly), and a script that sounds spoken, not read.
- **Longer proposal / business-plan pitch section** (1–2 pages): same structure and same plain-language rules, just with room to back claims with more evidence. More space is not permission to add hedging or jargon back in — it's room for more proof.

## Before handing it back: the read-aloud checklist

Run the draft against these before calling it done:

- Could someone repeat the core idea back in one sentence after a single read? (The 15-second test.)
- Any phrase here that could appear in literally any other company's pitch? Cut it or make it specific to this one.
- Does the problem feel like something a real person actually experiences, or does it stay abstract?
- Is every big claim backed by a real number, or clearly flagged as a placeholder to fill in?
- Does it end with a specific ask and next step, not a vague "let's talk more"?
- Is anything overstated in a way that wouldn't survive a follow-up question? Confident and honest beats hyped and shaky — an investor's very next move is almost always to test the claim.
